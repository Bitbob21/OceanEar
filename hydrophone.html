The hydrophone

Aquarian Audio H2d · specifications

The OceanEar kit ships an Aquarian Audio H2d, built by Aquarian Audio & Scientific in Anacortes, Washington. It is not our own transducer, and we would rather name it than be vague about it: it is a known quantity in field recording and marine research, and you can look up what other people have done with one.

Why this hydrophone

Low self-noise and good sensitivity across the audible range, in a housing that survives being dropped on a wharf. The H2d uses Aquarian’s CS6 signal conditioning board, which the manufacturer rates at roughly 10 dB better sensitivity and signal-to-noise than the CS4 board in the previous H2a.

Its specific gravity of 5.3 matters more than it sounds: the capsule is dense enough to sink and stay put in a current instead of drifting up and slapping against the hull, which is most of what ruins an underwater recording.

Figures published by Aquarian Audio for typical response. A hydrophone on plug-in power depends on the device it is plugged into: gain and high-frequency behaviour follow the preamp’s bias current and input impedance, and the cable length. That is why the manufacturer publishes no response plots, and why the kit ships a known interface rather than leaving it to whatever socket you happen to have.

What the sensitivity number means

−172 dB re 1V/µPa reads as: one micropascal of sound pressure produces an output 172 dB below one volt. Hydrophone sensitivities are negative by convention, and closer to zero means more sensitive.

Note the linear range in that figure. The useful range is quoted so wide because these hydrophones are also used to detect high-amplitude ultrasound such as acoustic beacons; sensitivity falls a long way outside the linear band, to roughly −220 dB at 100 kHz. For ocean sound in the audible range, that ceiling is not something you will meet.

Depth is the real limit. Below about 80 metres the housing is out of its rating. For everything a phone-based kit is realistically doing — a wharf, a dinghy, the shoreline, a dive line — that is far more depth than cable.

In the OceanEar kit

The kit pairs the hydrophone with a class-compliant USB-C interface, so it arrives ready for an iPhone with nothing else to buy, plus a redeem code for the full app. The interface is the part that matters here: it supplies the plug-in power the hydrophone needs and bypasses the phone’s combined headset jack, which is not wired for a microphone like this one.

Choose 3 m or 6 m of cable when you order. Three metres suits a wharf, a jetty or the side of a dinghy; six gives you room to get the capsule below the surface layer and away from hull noise. Other lengths are available — ask before ordering. Remember that cable length also affects high-frequency response.

The kit specifications cover the recording side: 48 kHz, WAV or AAC, GPS and timestamp per file.
